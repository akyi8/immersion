# Story Structure Guidance

This file holds the guidance for Immersion's three story structures. Each structure opens with a short overview, which the app shows when the structure is chosen. Each part of a structure has a paragraph on what the part does and a few prompts for outlining it, and the app shows both when the writer opens the arrow on that part's header. In Four Acts, each act also lists the Hero's Journey stages that can happen inside it, recommended first and optional after. The app reads this file as written, so a heading, a paragraph, or a prompt changed here changes on the board.

The guidance draws on the Shine Storytelling Engine and on Radiance's stage cards.

## Kishōtenketsu

Kishōtenketsu is a four-part structure from East Asian storytelling, built around a situation to be understood rather than a problem to be beaten. It needs no conflict and no villain. Its drama comes from pacing and from a great twist that arrives in the middle of the story rather than near the end. The characters do not have to change, because the change can happen in the reader instead.

### Ki

Ki introduces the characters, their world, and the feeling of the story. Ki needs no inciting incident. Its hook is a compelling sense of who these people are and what the story will feel like to live inside. Ki is also the place to plant small details that the twist will later turn upside down.

- Who does the reader meet first, and what makes them worth following?
- What feeling should the reader carry out of Ki?
- What small detail here could the twist later turn upside down?

### Shō

Shō deepens the reader's understanding of the situation and their attachment to the characters. The drama builds, but nothing fundamental changes yet. If the twist needs groundwork, Shō is where to bury it, quietly enough that the reader does not notice it being laid.

- What does the reader come to believe here that the twist will overturn?
- How does the energy build while the world stays the same?
- Where is the groundwork for the twist hidden?

### Ten

Ten is the twist, and it is the emotional climax of the story. Something unexpected upends everything, and the characters learn that the story they were in is not the story they thought. The twist can be a surprise from outside, or it can be the painful revelation of a fault, failing, or bias in someone the reader trusted. Conflict, if the story has any, often appears for the first time here.

- What did the characters, and the reader, believe in Ki and Shō that turns out to be wrong?
- Is the twist a surprise from outside or a revelation from within?
- How does Ten mirror something from Ki?
- Why can this not be happening, and why is it happening anyway?

### Ketsu

Ketsu reconciles what was believed with what was discovered. It sets the before and the after side by side, so the reader can see the whole of the journey at once, and it lets everything make at least a little sense. Ketsu does not have to resolve every tension, and it may face a new problem that the twist created.

- How do the characters make sense of the twist, even partly?
- What from Ki looks different now?
- What consequence of the twist must be faced before the end?
- Which tension is it honest to leave unresolved?

## Story Circle

The Story Circle is Dan Harmon's eight steps, drawn from the Hero's Journey and simplified into a loop. A character wants something, gets it, pays for it, and is changed. The first half of the circle travels into the unfamiliar and finds what was wanted, and the second half pays the price and comes home. Read in pairs, the eight steps make four acts. The circle works for a whole book, for a single act, for one character's arc, or for a single scene.

### You

The circle begins with the main character in their familiar situation. You establishes who they are when nothing much is happening, which gives the reader a person to follow and a starting point to measure the change against.

- Who is this person on an ordinary day?
- What is their comfort zone, and what keeps them in it?
- What will the reader compare them to at the end?

### Need

The character wants something. The want is what pulls them out of the familiar, and it is often not the same as what they truly need.

- What do they want, in concrete terms?
- Why can they not have it where they are?
- What do they need that they do not yet know about?

### Go

The character crosses into an unfamiliar situation to get what they want. Go is a threshold, and the world on the other side works by different rules.

- What decision or event takes them across the line?
- What is different on the other side?
- What did they leave behind to go?

### Search

The character adapts to the unfamiliar situation and is tested on the way to what they want. Search is often the longest stretch of the story.

- What trials teach them the rules of the new situation?
- Who helps them, and who stands in the way?
- How do they change their methods as they go?

### Find

The character gets what they wanted, in whole or in part. Find usually lands near the midpoint, and it often feels like a victory.

- What exactly do they get?
- Is it what they expected it to be?
- What does getting it cost that they cannot see yet?

### Take

The desire backfires, or it demands payment. Take is where the price of getting what they wanted comes due.

- What does the desire cost them?
- Who else pays for it?
- What do they lose that they cannot get back?

### Return

The character heads back toward the familiar, carrying the consequences with them. Return is about adapting: repairing mistakes, solving the problems that the desire created, and seeing home through new eyes.

- What do they bring back with them?
- What must they fix on the way?
- How do they see the familiar world differently now?

### Change

The circle closes on a character transformed by what happened. Change answers the question You asked at the start, by showing who they are now.

- What have they learned?
- How do they act differently than they did in You?
- What closing image shows the change?

## Four Acts

Four Acts is the Hero's Journey told in Shine's four movements: The Beginning, The Middle, The Crucible, and The Resolution. Each act holds a set of stages. The recommended stages carry most stories, and the optional stages fit some stories and not others. The stages are not equal in size. The Road of Trials can fill a third of a book while the Herald takes one scene or none, so a story uses the stages it needs and lets each one run as long as it runs. A useful order for planning is Act I first, then Act IV, then Act III, and Act II last.

### Act I: The Beginning

Act I establishes the world and then breaks it. The reader meets the main character in their ordinary world, an event shatters that world and reveals the core story problem, and the character is called to act.

- Who is the main character before the story touches them?
- What shatters their ordinary world, and what problem does it reveal?
- What are they called to do, and how do they answer?

**Recommended stages**
- **Calm Before the Storm.** The main character and the ordinary world they live in, seen through the eyes the reader will borrow for the rest of the story.
- **Cataclysm.** The event that shatters the ordinary world and reveals the core story problem.
- **Call to Adventure.** The moment the main character steps outside the status quo and acts.

**Optional stages**
- **Prologue.** Tone, theme, or foreshadowing before the reader meets the main character. Leave it out without a compelling reason.
- **Herald.** Someone or something that warns the ordinary life is about to end.
- **Threshold.** Crossing from the known world into the new one, often through a first test of commitment.

### Act II: The Middle

Act II explores and escalates. Something about the character's reality is now different, and that difference matters. The character acts on the core story problem by exploring, investigating, gathering strength, and trying to solve it, and they learn and grow even as they fail. The act often ends on a hope that will not survive Act III.

- What is different now, and why does that difference matter?
- How does the character try to solve the problem without succeeding?
- What do they learn by trying?
- What victory seems within reach as the act ends?

**Recommended stages**
- **The Extraordinary World.** The changed reality, whether the world itself has changed or only the character's view of it.
- **Road of Trials.** A series of actions toward the core story problem: exploring, testing, gathering strength. It is often the longest stretch of the book.
- **False Summits.** An apparent victory near the end of the act that will prove false.

**Optional stages**
- **Planning.** Someone or something tells the character that their efforts are misdirected and that they need a plan.
- **Scale of the Thing.** A potent reminder of how large, complex, or dangerous the core story problem still is.

### Act III: The Crucible

Act III destroys and transforms. It opens with the rug pulled out: a reveal, a twist, or a reversal that resets the story's direction. Things grow harder and darker until the character is stripped of their misconceptions and must face the great change at the heart of their inner journey.

- How does the story change direction: a setback, a twist, or a revelation?
- What makes this the hardest part of the story?
- What misconceptions are stripped away?
- What great change or lesson must the character accept?

**Recommended stages**
- **Undone.** The reveal, twist, or reversal that resets the tone and direction of the story.
- **Descent into the Cave.** Everything is harder, stranger, or darker than before.
- **Dark Night of the Soul.** The character is beaten down and stripped bare, ready for the great change.
- **End of the Inner Journey.** The character accepts the great change, learns the great lesson, or reconciles the great truth.

**Optional stages**
- **Radicalization.** Pressure drives the character into a warrior's mindset or another extreme.
- **Pertinax Innocentia.** The character refuses to accept the reversal, or that their earlier goals were misguided.
- **Induratus Stultum.** The character stubbornly refuses the coming lesson or transformation.
- **Mors Certa.** Expecting to fail, the character chooses to go down fighting.
- **Desperatio.** Despair so deep that the character considers abandoning the story.
- **Gemini.** Another character, often the opposing force, shares the same inner journey and makes the opposite choice.
- **Paragon.** The character meets a flawed ideal they admire, until they discover the flaw.

### Act IV: The Resolution

Act IV earns the ending. The character comes out of Act III reborn, often carrying a gift from that ordeal, and faces the core story problem one last time. The story then closes the threads that need closing.

- What does the character carry out of Act III that helps them now?
- How do they face the core story problem for the last time?
- Do they succeed, and what does it cost?
- Which threads must close, and which may honestly stay open?

**Recommended stages**
- **Resurrection & Boon.** The character is reborn from Act III, with a new opportunity and often a benefit gained from the ordeal.
- **The Final Battle.** The last effort against the core story problem, the opposing force, or both.
- **Resolutions.** The threads of the story are brought to their ends.

**Optional stages**
- **End of the Extraordinary World.** Resolving the problem destroys the extraordinary world, sometimes literally, to make way for a new ordinary one.
- **Refusal of the Return.** The character does not want the adventure to end.
- **Master of Two Worlds.** The veteran struggles to belong in the new ordinary world they helped make.
- **The Return.** The resolution itself puts the character in new danger.
- **Tragedy.** The story ends in failure, death, or mortification.
